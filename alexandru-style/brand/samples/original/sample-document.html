The Long View

Most engineering decisions outlive the teams that make them. The teams that survive are the ones that learn to see that far — and to keep the habit of writing down why they decided.

Alexandru Gris Engineering Leader Munich 9 min read

Every team I have joined has been solving a version of the same problem: not the one written in the roadmap, but the one underneath it. The roadmap says ship the feature. The real problem is almost always about how the work is organised — who decides, what gets measured, and what happens when two good engineers disagree.

I have spent my career building software, products, and teams across countries and industries. The technology changed constantly. The pattern did not. Teams that treat engineering as a delivery problem produce more code and learn less; teams that treat it as a learning problem produce less code, sooner, and understand why.

The work that matters is the work that is still true in two years. Notes from a review, 2024

This is not an argument for slowness. It is an argument for choosing the work that compounds: a codebase a team understands gets faster every quarter.

Where the time goes

Observation

Ask a team where its engineering time goes and you will get three different answers in one meeting. None of them are wrong. What is missing is the bottom of the distribution — the slice of time that keeps a team from merely repeating itself.

Four principles, still standing

Management · 2025
01 Disagree — and then commit.

Open disagreement is the cheapest quality control there is. Once the call is made, the team moves as one.

02 Authority is not an argument.

Seniority decides who is accountable, not who is right. Bring evidence and expect it weighed the same way.

03 Move fast, focused, and deep.

Speed without focus is churn. Speed with depth compounds: the team gets faster at what it already knows.

04 Measure learning and process.

Output metrics reward the wrong behaviour. Measure how the team learns and how the work flows.

None of these are new, and none of them are clever. That is the point: principles earn their place by surviving contact with reality.

What measurement is actually for

Method

Most engineering metrics were built to be reported upward, not acted upon. The honest ones are narrow and slightly uncomfortable.

SignalWhat it tells youWhat it does not tell you
Deployment frequencyHow fast changes landWhether the changes were right
Review turnaroundWhere the queue actually sitsThat review quality is good
Defect escape rateWhere quality leaks outThat the system is well designed
Learning hoursWhether the team is still growingThat growth happened
A metric you cannot act on is decoration. A metric you act on weekly is culture.

What we stopped measuring

Cuts
  • Sprint velocityA planning aid until it becomes a target — and then it stops describing the work.
  • Lines of codeVolume is not difficulty. Counting it rewards the wrong code and hides the hard parts.
  • Hours workedIt measures presence instead of progress, and it quietly punishes people who think best alone.

A note on disagreement

Practice

Most teams do not fail from too little disagreement. They fail from disagreement that leaks — into hallways, into status meetings, into late-night pull requests that nobody wanted to write. The fix is not more harmony. It is a container for the argument.

Separate the person from the position
Nobody is defending their ego when the question is framed as evidence. Say what the evidence is, and say what would change your mind.
Argue about the work, not the rank
Authority decides who is accountable for the call. It does not decide who is correct. Write the reasoning down so the next person can check it.
Commit in public, once
After the call, the team moves as one. Reopening the decision is legitimate when new evidence appears — not because someone did not win the room.
Decision record — the smallest useful artefact 
decision: 2026-03-11 — split the ingestion service
context:  single pipeline became the bottleneck at 3x traffic
options:  scale the pipeline | split by source | queue + batch
choice:   split by source
because:  smallest change that removes the shared bottleneck
owner:    platform team — review in 90 days
revise_if: p95 latency exceeds 400ms after the split

The last line is the one that matters. A decision without a condition for revisiting is a decision that will quietly rot. Writing one down costs five minutes and buys the team a shared memory it would otherwise rebuild from scratch.

The short version

Summary

Write the decision down. Name the condition that would make you revisit it. Keep a slice of the team's time for learning, and defend it. Do the disagreeing in the room, where it is cheap, rather than in the hallway, where it is expensive.

None of this is slow. It is the difference between a team that ships more each quarter and a team that is still standing in the same place next year.
